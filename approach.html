How Inference Works

This page explains the approach behind ConnInfPy — the reasoning that connects “connectivity matrices and a hypothesis” to “these edges are significant.” It is conceptual rather than formal: the aim is to make clear why each step is present. For the recipes (which function to call) see Recommended Workflows; for the equations and citations see References.

The package applies a single inferential scheme across designs:

a statistic at each edge  →  network enhancement  →
a null distribution from relabelling  →  comparison of observed to null

The remainder of this page covers each of these four steps and the reasoning behind it.

Rationale for permutation testing

A connectome has many edges (\(N(N{-}1)/2\)), and they are not independent: neighbouring regions covary, hubs participate in many edges, and the correlations are bounded and non-normal. A parametric per-edge p-value assumes a known distribution and independent tests; neither holds here, and across thousands of edges even a small per-edge error rate yields many false positives.

Permutation testing avoids both assumptions. Rather than assume a null distribution, the method constructs one: the data are relabelled so that the effect is removed, the full statistic is recomputed, and the process is repeated. The only requirement is exchangeability — under the null, the relabelled data are as probable as the observed data. This is a substantially weaker assumption than normality, and the resulting null automatically reflects the empirical covariance among edges.

Step 1 — a statistic at each edge

At every edge a single statistic quantifies the strength and reliability of the effect. The choice of statistic follows the design:

  • Two groups → a t-statistic of the difference in means: Welch’s by default (separate group variances), or Student’s with var_equal=True (pooled variance, as in the MATLAB NBS toolbox). The group-label permutation is the same for both; with equal group sizes they coincide.

  • Same subjects, two conditions → a one-sample t of the within-subject differences.

  • A predictor with confounds (e.g. age, controlling for motion and sex) → a linear model fit at each edge, reading off the t for the predictor of interest.

Direction is meaningful, so ConnInfPy always splits the statistic into two separate tails — positive (e.g. group2 > group1, or predictor ↑ → connectivity ↑) and negative — and propagates them independently. Testing a single two-sided statistic would allow a positive and a negative block to cancel.

from conninfpy import compute_t_stat, fisher_r_to_z
t = compute_t_stat(fisher_r_to_z(g1), fisher_r_to_z(g2),
                   test_type='two-sample')
# t['g2>g1'] is the per-edge statistic for "group2 > group1"

Step 1 thus reduces the data to one strength-map per direction.

Step 2 — network enhancement

A connectivity effect rarely appears as a single isolated edge; it typically appears as a connected set of edges that covary. An isolated edge with a large statistic may be noise, whereas a coherent set of moderate edges is more convincing evidence. Enhancement rewrites each edge’s score to reflect the network structure it participates in; the operators differ in how they define and summarise that structure.

A useful way to picture enhancement: take the strength-map from step 1 and progressively raise a threshold (a “water level”). At a low threshold most edges are supra-threshold and form large connected components; as the threshold rises, only the strongest edges remain, in small components. The operators differ in how they read these components.

No enhancement — tstat

Each edge is tested on its own, with no network aggregation. Useful as a baseline and for strong focal effects, but insensitive to the distributed weak effects typical of brain connectivity — which the operators below are designed to recover.

Fixed threshold — nbs

Classical Network-Based Statistics. Fix a single threshold, identify the connected components above it, and score each edge by the size of its component (extent) or by the summed excess of the statistic over the threshold, \(\sum (t - \tau)\), within it (intensity — only the excess above \(\tau\) counts, so an edge just above the threshold adds almost nothing and a small cluster of strong edges can outrank a large cluster of weak ones). It is most powerful when the effect forms a large connected component above the chosen threshold. The threshold is arbitrary, however, and results can vary substantially with it (Vinokur et al. 2023 report up to 75-fold variation in detected edges across reasonable thresholds); report results at several thresholds when using it.

The inference unit is the component. Its statistic is compared with the permutation distribution of the largest component statistic (family-wise error control over components; see Step 4): with two_tailed=True (the analyze() default) the two tails share one null, the larger of the two tails’ maxima in every permutation; with separate one-sided families (explicit two_tailed=False) each tail has its own null of maxima. Every edge of a component carries the component’s p-value, so a significant component is a claim about the set, not about each of its edges. result.component_report (and result.component_table(atlas)) lists every observed component of each tail: its component_id (numbered within the tail by decreasing statistic), edges, ROIs, extent or intensity, and p_fwer; with an atlas also the ROI names and the networks and network pairs it spans. Components that are not significant are listed too (significant marks p_fwer <= alpha); a single suprathreshold edge is a component of one edge. The component_id column of significant_edges() and of the CSV export gives the component of each edge. The p-values are those of the p-value map: the report recomputes nothing. result.node_summary(atlas) describes the same detections per ROI (which ROIs gather them): for edge-wise methods the significant edges of each ROI out of its tested incident edges; for nbs and cnbs, whose edges in a component or block share one p-value, the significant components or blocks containing the ROI and how many of its edges lie in them, never a count of “significant edges” or a mass of -log10 p. It is descriptive and adds no significance.

Threshold-free — tfnbs (the default)

Rather than fix a single threshold, TFNBS integrates the cluster evidence over the full range of thresholds:

\[\text{score}(e) \;=\; \int_{h_0}^{w_e} (\text{edge count in the component containing } e \text{ at } h)^{E}\, h^{H}\, dh\]

At low thresholds an edge accrues weight from belonging to a spatially extended component; at high thresholds from its own magnitude. Summing across thresholds rewards an edge that is extended or strong, removing the dependence on any single cutoff. E controls the contribution of component size and H that of statistic height; they act as sensitivity parameters rather than fixed constants, and can be swept in one permutation pass to report stability (Inference stability: sweep (E, H) in one call). This is the recommended default when there is no strong prior about where the effect is located.

Using a known network partition — cnbs, ni_tfnbs, fbc_tfnbs

Given an atlas partition (e.g. Yeo-7 networks, lobes, or modules), the operator can be directed to expect effects that respect those blocks:

  • cnbs (constrained NBS; Noble & Scheinost, 2020) — averages the signed edge statistic over every edge of each within- and between-network block, tests each block against its own permutation null (the same block’s mean in every permuted map), and corrects the block p-values across blocks (cnbs_correction='bonferroni' for FWER, the default, or 'bh' for FDR). The inference unit is the block: every edge of a significant block carries the block’s p-value, and result.block_report lists the blocks. A significant block means its mean effect is nonzero, not that each of its edges is affected; edges that go in opposite directions cancel. Most powerful for block-aligned effects such as within-network hypoconnectivity; it can miss effects that are small parts of large blocks. With K blocks and Bonferroni the smallest attainable p-value is K/(B+1), so use enough permutations.

  • ni_tfnbs (network-informed, soft prior) — integrates an edge-level block-density score over thresholds, without connected-component clustering. At threshold h, k_b(h) eligible edges are active in block b and the extent weight is k_b(h)/sqrt(M_b) by default (normalization='linear' uses k_b(h)/M_b; 'none' uses k_b(h)). M_b is the fixed number of eligible undirected opportunities: n_a*n_b between networks and n_a*(n_a-1)/2 within a network when all pairs are eligible. Available-case inference excludes untested pairs from both capacity and active counts. The same capacity is used for observed and permuted maps. Its behavior depends on the partition; a misspecified partition does not generally reproduce plain TFNBS.

  • fbc_tfnbs (functional-block-clustering, hard prior) — treats each block as a single atomic cluster and requires a minimum number of active edges per block. It targets diffuse, block-confined effects that are spread too thinly to form a strong connected component.

Because the choice encodes an assumption, the recommended practice is to report two operators: a topology-agnostic reference (tfnbs) and the network-aware operator matching the prior. Agreement between them strengthens a finding; disagreement is itself informative about the effect’s spatial shape.

Step 3 — the null distribution from relabelling (permutation schemes)

Given the enhanced score-map from the observed data, its significance is assessed against the distribution expected under the null. That distribution is constructed by relabelling the data so as to remove the effect while preserving all other structure, rerunning steps 1–2, and repeating many times to obtain a collection of null score-maps.

The principle underlying every scheme is exchangeability: under the null, certain labels are interchangeable, and a valid permutation is any reshuffle that respects that interchangeability. The correct scheme is the relabelling that removes exactly the tested effect while leaving every nuisance structure intact — too little randomisation leaves the effect in the null (low power); too much scrambles structure that should be held fixed (mis-calibrated false-positive rate). Each design has its own scheme.

Two groups — shuffle the group labels

Removes: the association between subject and group label. Preserves: each subject’s connectivity matrix. Under the null the two groups are exchangeable, so the observed assignment is one of many equally probable assignments; the null is generated by drawing other assignments and recomputing.

Paired conditions — sign-flip each subject’s difference

Removes: the direction of each within-subject change. Preserves: the pairing and each subject’s magnitude. The response is the per-subject difference B − A (group2 − group1) on the analysis scale — for correlations, Fisher z of each condition before the subtraction. If the two conditions are interchangeable within each subject, the labelling of each subject’s difference as “A−B” or “B−A” is equiprobable, so each subject’s difference matrix is randomly negated, subject by subject, independently.

What the null needs is an invariance of the joint law: the stacked difference matrices of all subjects must have the same distribution after any pattern of per-subject sign flips. Independent subjects whose difference matrices are centrally symmetric (each matrix distributed like its own negation) satisfy this; within-subject exchangeability of A and B is one way to get there. Unequal variances of A and B do not by themselves break it — a symmetric difference can have asymmetric condition marginals — and nothing else about the marginal laws is assumed. Without the invariance, exactness and Type-I control of the sign-flip test are not guaranteed, and a condition-varying covariate does not repair that (the Freedman–Lane route below rests on its own, approximate, premise). With all \(2^n\) sign patterns enumerated the test is exact under the invariance; the engine samples patterns at random, which gives the Monte Carlo form of Step 4. For edge-wise methods (tstat, the TFNBS family) the hypothesis is about the mean difference: a significant edge means that the average within-subject change differs from zero, not that every subject changes.

Predictor with confounds — Freedman–Lane

A natural but incorrect approach is to shuffle the predictor of interest directly. When the predictor is correlated with the confounds (e.g. age with motion), shuffling it also destroys that correlation, so the permuted data no longer match the model under the null and the test is mis-calibrated. Freedman–Lane permutes only the part of the data the confounds cannot account for:

  1. Fit the reduced model (confounds only). Decompose each edge’s data into the fitted part (explained by the confounds) and the residual.

  2. Permute the residuals, then add the fitted part back, unchanged.

  3. Recompute the predictor’s statistic on the reconstructed data.

Removes: any genuine predictor effect (the residuals are now in random subject order). Preserves: the confound structure, held fixed. Only the residual — the component of the data the predictor of interest could explain — is permuted, so the resulting null isolates the tested effect while controlling the confounds.

Repeated measures with condition-varying confounds

The within-subject difference is formed first. Any quantity constant per subject — including additive site effects — cancels in the subtraction. Freedman–Lane is then applied to the differences to partial out the component of the confound that varies between conditions. The two adjustments are applied in the order that makes each straightforward.

Several predictors at once

Each predictor is tested against its own Freedman–Lane null: its reduced model keeps the other predictors as nuisance, so a real effect of one predictor stays in the fitted part while another is tested. The permutations are shared across predictors, so each result equals a separate single-predictor run with the same seed (see Several predictors in one pass). A single reduced model that removes every predictor at once would test the joint null “all of them are zero” instead, which is conservative when one of them has an effect.

Multiple sites — permute within site

Subjects from different scanners are not freely exchangeable, since a site can shift the connectivity baseline. Passing sites= confines every permutation to occur within each site block, never across blocks. Removes: the effect, within each site. Preserves: the between-site differences, which therefore remain in the null rather than leaking into the effect and inflating false positives. This is the permutation component of multi-site handling; ComBat (below) is the complementary harmonization component.

from conninfpy import compute_p_val, compute_p_val_glm, fisher_r_to_z
import numpy as np

# two groups: group labels are permuted internally
compute_p_val(g1, g2, test_type='two-sample', method='tfnbs', rng=42)

# paired: each subject's difference is sign-flipped internally (Monte Carlo sign-flip null)
compute_p_val(rest, task, test_type='paired', method='tfnbs', rng=42)

# predictor + confounds: Freedman–Lane handles the relabelling
compute_p_val_glm(fisher_r_to_z(Y), interest=age,
                  confounds=np.column_stack([sex, motion]),
                  method='tfnbs', rng=42)

# multi-site: pass sites= and permutation stays within each site block
compute_p_val_glm(fisher_r_to_z(Y), interest=age,
                  confounds=np.column_stack([sex, motion]),
                  sites=site, method='tfnbs', rng=42)

Step 4 — comparison of observed to null

The max-statistic methods — tstat, the TFNBS family and nbs — retain a single value from each relabelled null map: the maximum enhanced score over all edges (for nbs the enhancement has already copied each component’s statistic onto its edges, so this is the largest component statistic). Across permutations these maxima form the null distribution of the largest score attributable to noise alone. A unit is declared significant when its corrected p-value (the rule below) is at most the chosen α, and that p-value is written onto every edge of the unit. The unit is result.inference_unit: 'edge' for tstat and the TFNBS family, 'component' for nbs. The p-value of a unit counts the permutation maxima that match or exceed its statistic, with the observed statistic added to both the count and the number of permutations — not a bare proportion:

\[p(e) \;=\; \frac{1 + \#\{\text{permutations whose max} \ge \text{score}(e)\}} {1 + (\text{number of permutations})}\]

Two methods stand outside this formula. cnbs compares every block’s statistic with that block’s own permutation null (the same block’s value in every permuted map, in absolute value under two_tailed=True) and then corrects the block p-values across blocks with Bonferroni (FWER over blocks) or Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR over blocks) — given valid marginal block p-values, single-step Bonferroni bounds the FWER over blocks strongly, without any subset-pivotality argument, and BH gives FDR over blocks under its own premises; its unit is 'block'. bh_fdr_perm keeps a per-edge marginal permutation p-value and applies Benjamini–Hochberg across the eligible edges; bh_fdr and bonferroni are parametric and use no permutations.

Three design choices are embedded in the formula:

  • Why the maximum? Comparing every unit to the distribution of the largest null score keeps the probability of any false positive at the inference unit at most α under the global null (no effect anywhere) — weak family-wise error control — without requiring knowledge of the inter-edge covariance, which is already reflected in the permutations. Whether a p-value ≤ α means this in the data at hand — here and for the FDR variants — depends on the permutation scheme being valid: the relabelling of Step 3 must leave the data, under the null, distributed as the observed data, and the reconstructed null maps must reproduce the sampling law of the statistics. Sign-flip and group-label permutation do this under their exchangeability premises; Freedman–Lane is an approximation (Winkler et al. 2014), so GLM p-values computed with Freedman–Lane inherit that approximation (the argument does not extend to other valid schemes a GLM could use). Strong control (the same bound under any mix of true and false nulls) needs more: the joint distribution of the true-null units’ statistics must be the same whatever the other units are — subset pivotality (Westfall & Troendle 2008; Rempala & Yang 2013) — on top of a valid scheme. Invariance of each marginal null law alone is not enough, and an edge-wise t by itself does not establish it for these statistics; for the TFNBS family an edge’s enhanced score depends on its neighbours’ statistics, and for nbs the unit is the component, so a significant component is a claim about the set and never about each of its edges. No unconditional guarantee of strong control is given for this max-statistic path; read detections at result.inference_unit, which component_report / component_table and node_summary keep.

  • Why the +1? The observed statistic is itself one admissible relabelling and is counted (Phipson & Smyth 2010; Hemerik & Goeman 2017). With relabellings drawn at random with replacement the test is a Monte Carlo test: the +1 rule keeps it valid at level α given a valid scheme and keeps the p-value strictly positive, whereas the naive proportion is slightly liberal. It does not turn a sampled null into the enumerated one — exhaustive enumeration of all relabellings is a different procedure, exact under its premises, and is not what the engine does.

To control the false-discovery rate instead (greater sensitivity at the cost of some false positives), method='bh_fdr_perm' replaces the max-statistic step with a per-edge permutation p-value and a Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Auxiliary components: harmonization and acceleration

ComBat (multi-site harmonization). Different scanners introduce additive and multiplicative offsets that can exceed the biological signal. ComBat estimates and removes these per-site offsets before step 1, while preserving the covariates of interest, and excludes the tested variable from its model so that the harmonization never incorporates the labels the permutation will later shuffle (which would otherwise inflate false positives). The site strategies and their trade-offs are described in Multi-site GLM with ComBat harmonization.

Acceleration (GPD / gamma tail, opt-in). With J permutations the empirical p-value of a unit rests on how many null maxima reach its statistic, and its resolution is 1/(J+1). acceleration='gpd' (default None) fits a generalized Pareto distribution to the exceedances over a sequence of thresholds, starting at the upper quartile of the null maxima and moving upwards until a method-of-moments fit passes an Anderson–Darling check (Winkler et al., 2016; Knijnenburg et al., 2009); acceleration='gamma' fits a gamma distribution to all null maxima by their moments. Either replaces the p-value only of units that fewer than 10 null maxima reach (for the GPD, only units at or above the fitted threshold); every other p-value stays the empirical permutation p-value, a failed fit leaves the empirical p in place, and no binomial interval is reported for an accelerated result. The replaced values are model-based tail estimates that can be smaller than 1/(J+1); their accuracy is not established by the number of permutations, and the option exists for exploration with a few hundred permutations — reported results use the empirical default. Known limits of the 2.0 implementation: a replaced p-value is capped at min(1, (min_exceedances + 1)/(J + 1)), which can be larger than the empirical p-value of the same unit — the replacement can raise a p, for GPD and gamma alike; the gamma branch has no goodness-of-fit check and no eligibility filter, and a statistic below its fitted support gets a tail survival function of 1, recorded as that cap — which is 1 when J equals min_exceedances (reproduced in a helper-level example with J = 10; not observed at J = 200/1000 in a limited check); with a bounded (ξ < 0) GPD fit, one unit beyond the fitted endpoint cancels the fit for the whole matrix at that threshold, and when no threshold is accepted the empirical p-values are returned unchanged; in the GLM and missing-data paths the acceleration is applied to every statistic type without a mode check, unlike the pairwise max-statistic path; and with method='cnbs' it is not applied (a warning is logged). The Anderson–Darling threshold 2.5 is a lenient heuristic filter that has not been calibrated for the method-of-moments fit, and the moment conditions on the shape parameter are not checked. No FWER, power or anti-conservativeness claim is made for accelerated p-values.

Directional families. two_tailed=True declares one two-sided family for pairwise and GLM inference. Max-statistic methods pool positive/negative null maxima, cNBS tests absolute block means before across-block correction, and pairwise BH methods correct two-sided marginal p-values across eligible edges. Directional maps retain the p-value in the observed direction and 1 in the opposite direction. analyze() defaults to this two-sided family: one nominal α applies to the error measure of the chosen method — FWER for the max-statistic methods and bonferroni, and for cnbs with cnbs_correction='bonferroni'; FDR for the BH variants and for cnbs with cnbs_correction='bh' — over both directions jointly, and only under that measure’s premises (above). Its two_tailed=False keeps the legacy separate one-sided families, each corrected at the nominal α; two families at α = 0.05 do not promise a combined-direction FWER of 0.05 — for FWER, α/2 per tail bounds the combined FWER at α by Bonferroni (under the same premises); this is not an FDR statement. The low-level compute_p_val() / compute_p_val_glm() entry-points also default to two_tailed=True.

For a single prespecified direction (e.g. controls > patients, as in the MATLAB NBS toolbox’s t-test), declare it before the run: analyze(..., tested_tail='positive') (or 'negative'). The run uses per-tail nulls, the tested tail keeps its own-null p-values at the nominal alpha, the other direction is reported as not tested (p = 1), and the result records tail_policy = 'one-sided-positive' with tail_selection = 'prespecified', which provenance, exports and the app carry through. Choosing a direction only after inspecting a finished run is a post hoc selection: result.one_sided('positive') performs it and is marked tail_selection = 'post-hoc' instead, so a restriction made after seeing the p-values is never reported as a hypothesis fixed in advance. Each enhancement setting and contrast is corrected separately; selecting among settings or contrasts requires an additional declared correction.

End-to-end example

A single analyze() call runs all four steps, with ComBat and within-site permutation included:

from conninfpy import analyze
import numpy as np

out = analyze(
    Y,                                   # raw correlations (Fisher-z applied)
    interest=diagnosis,                  # step 1: a t at each edge
    confounds=np.column_stack([age, sex, mean_fd]),
    sites=site,                          # ComBat + within-site permutation
    harmonize='combat_site_dummies_glm',
    method='tfnbs',                      # step 2: threshold-free enhancement
    n_permutations=5000, acceleration=None,   # steps 3–4: empirical null
    rng=42,
)
out.inference['positive']                # FWER p-map, diagnosis ↑ → conn ↑

The call composes the four steps: a t-statistic at each edge (controlling for age, sex, and motion) → TFNBS enhancement over connected components → a within-site Freedman–Lane null on the harmonized data → a family-wise-corrected p-value from the max-statistic distribution with the +1 correction. See Recommended Workflows for the design variants and References for the supporting literature.

After inference: anatomical reading

The four steps end with corrected p-values on the parcellation the signals were measured with. Reading the result anatomically (conninfpy.interpret; Anatomical interpretation — conninfpy.interpret) is a descriptive layer that changes none of them:

  • The tissue, not the centre point. Each ROI is described by its overlap with the 246 regions of the Brainnetome atlas (Fan et al., 2016) over the sphere or parcel that produced its signal. A 6 mm sphere usually covers 2–4 Brainnetome regions and a Schaefer parcel often spans several subregions, so a label is given only at the finest level of the hierarchy (subregion → gyrus → lobe) that holds at least half of the ROI’s labelled tissue; below that the ROI is mixed.

  • The inference unit stays. Grouping detections by lobe, gyrus, circuit class, hemispheric or network relation re-describes them. It adds no test, and an edge of a significant component or block does not become individually significant.

  • Against what was tested. Detections are shown next to the tested opportunities in each category, because categories differ in size (there are far more cortico-cortical than thalamo-cortical pairs).

  • Literature context, not function. BrainMap behavioural-domain terms are forward-inference associations of each region in published activation studies (Laird et al., 2005); they are listed per region and never ranked into “the function” of a region or a connection.

Measuring the signals in Brainnetome regions (native parcellation) is a different analysis: it changes the signals and the tested edge family. examples/open_close/atlas_stability compares the two on the Open-Close contrast.
